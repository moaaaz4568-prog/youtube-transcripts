Look, when you're a product retailer, most people
take the easy way out: they just buy the product and sell it at a
higher price. But there are some retailers these days who
take a product, build an experience around it, and
then sell it. So, they're selling you, not a
car; they're selling you the experience of being a loyal customer of a certain
brand. Imagine
your car breaking down on the coastal road; you give a call, and
our team is there for you because you can't afford to be
inconvenienced. That's experience, not cars. When a
brand is just a product retailer, the opportunity is close
to half the available opportunities, if not more. But when they're a
product retailer and also
understand the business, creating an experience, they have a
huge advantage. You can then develop a craft,
meaning the service you provide surpasses the product itself. Service, by
its very nature, is built on expertise. So,
if you're a product retailer but understand the business
and have built an experience around it that matches the craft, you'll have
raw material equivalent to what's available at [a specific brand/market].
Your tradesperson—and by tradesperson, I don't necessarily mean a
mechanic, but a lawyer, a skilled tradesperson, or even a doctor—will have a
larger marketing scope. Not because they're more experienced, but because people
who have been playing the product game for the
past 50 years have been doing so as a business. But if product developers approached it as a business and gained experience, they'd be on par with service industry providers.
